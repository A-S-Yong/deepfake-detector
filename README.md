# deepfake-detector

## Introduction
Our deepfake detection web application leverages a multi modal approach to identify manipulated video content by combining cutting edge spatial and audio visual analysis techniques. By integrating advanced deep learning architectures such as the Swin Transformer, fine tuned Xception, ResNet 18, and Wav2Vec2, the system captures visual artifacts and audio visual misalignments that commonly arise in deepfake forgeries. Each model contributes unique strengths including frame level artifact detection, modality synchronization analysis, and ensemble learning to achieve high classification accuracy and robust generalization across diverse datasets. This web based solution offers a scalable and effective tool for real time detection of forged media, helping users safeguard against misinformation and media manipulation.

## Multi-Mdodal Approach
### Spatial Analysis
In our deepfake detection project, spatial analysis plays a critical role in identifying forged content based on visual inconsistencies within individual video frames. Unlike temporal or audio-visual analysis, which examine motion and sound over time, spatial analysis focuses solely on still-image features. Deepfake forgeries often introduce subtle artifacts such as unnatural facial blending, inconsistent lighting, or irregular textures that can be detected by sophisticated image classification models, even when they are imperceptible to the human eye.

Our spatial analysis module consists of two complementary deep learning models: the Swin Transformer and a fine-tuned version of the Xception model. Each model was selected for its unique strengths in visual representation learning and has been trained or adapted to suit the demands of frame-level forgery detection.

The first model, a Swin Transformer (swin_base_patch4_window7_224), was trained from scratch using extracted frames from manipulated and authentic videos. This architecture was chosen for its hierarchical attention mechanism, which computes local self-attention within non-overlapping windows and shifts them between layers to enable global context modeling. It has been shown to outperform traditional CNNs in various vision tasks. In our implementation, we froze the earliest layers to retain pre-learned low-level features and allowed the higher layers to adapt to our deepfake classification task. The model was trained on 224×224 resolution frames with standard normalization, and employed data augmentation techniques such as horizontal flipping and color jittering to improve generalizability. Optimization was performed using the AdamW optimizer, a low learning rate of 1e-5, and a step learning rate scheduler with early stopping to prevent overfitting.

Alongside the Swin Transformer, we incorporated a fine-tuned version of the Xception model adapted from the open-source PyDeepFakeDet framework. While the original pretrained Xception performed moderately on our dataset, we found that its accuracy improved significantly after selectively fine-tuning its last layers while keeping the feature extraction blocks frozen. The Xception model was trained using 256×256 input frames and optimized using AdamW. To ensure training stability and efficiency, we employed the ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler, which adapts the learning rate based on validation performance. This approach allowed us to tailor the model specifically to the type of spatial artifacts present in our data while minimizing the risk of overfitting.

Our training pipeline involved extracting ten evenly spaced frames from each video in the dataset using OpenCV. These frames were categorized into "real" and "fake" directories, and split into training (70%), validation (20%), and testing (10%) sets. Both models were trained using PyTorch and evaluated using standard classification metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. We also logged results to TensorBoard for monitoring convergence.

When evaluated individually, both models achieved competitive performance. The Swin Transformer demonstrated strong learning capability, benefiting from its attention-based architecture. The fine-tuned Xception, while lighter in computation, proved effective at capturing localized spatial anomalies. To further improve detection robustness, we implemented a soft-voting ensemble system that combines the outputs of both models. By assigning higher weight to the Swin Transformer and a complementary weight to Xception, we achieved improved overall classification accuracy on the test set compared to either model alone.

Despite its effectiveness, our spatial module does have limitations. As it relies on still images, it does not capture motion-based cues that may expose deepfakes in video sequences. Additionally, performance may degrade on heavily compressed videos or on manipulation techniques not well represented in the training data. The Swin Transformer also requires significant computational resources, particularly for inference at scale.

Nevertheless, spatial analysis remains a foundational component of our multimodal detection system. By focusing on visual inconsistencies frame-by-frame, it provides essential cues that complement the insights from our temporal and audio-visual models. Together, these models form a comprehensive detection pipeline capable of identifying forged media across multiple modalities.

This component builds upon publicly available resources, particularly the PyDeepFakeDet project, which provided a strong baseline for Xception. We acknowledge and appreciate the contributions of the authors of that work.

### Audio-Visual Analysis
In our deepfake detection framework, the audio-visual analysis component strives to uncover inconsistencies between a video’s visual content and its accompanying sound that betray forgery attempts. While spatial analysis focuses solely on individual frames and temporal analysis examines motion cues, audio-visual analysis exploits the natural synchronization between lip movements and speech patterns. Deepfake techniques often overlook the precise alignment of these modalities, leaving subtle mismatches that specialized models can detect.

To construct our audio-visual detector, we first consolidated multiple public and proprietary datasets—including Internet Sources, DeepfakeTIMIT, and Capstone repositories—into a unified archive. We flattened the directory hierarchy, removed videos lacking an audio track, and applied a 70/15/ 15 split for training, validation, and testing, respectively, ensuring that both genuine and manipulated samples were represented across each partition. This preprocessing pipeline automated frame extraction, spectrogram generation, and dataset organization, laying the groundwork for joint feature learning.

For the visual stream, we adopted a ResNet-18 backbone pretrained on ImageNet, truncating its final classification layer to produce 512-dimensional feature embeddings for each frame. Ten uniformly spaced frames per video were resized to 224×224 pixels, normalized, and optionally augmented with random flips and rotations during training. These frame-level embeddings were then averaged to yield a compact video representation. Simultaneously, the audio stream leveraged Facebook’s Wav2Vec2 model, a self-supervised transformer pretrained on large-scale speech corpora, to extract 768-dimensional embeddings from raw audio. We first converted each clip’s waveform into a Mel-spectrogram—log-scaled, standardized, and zero-padded or truncated to a fixed length—to align with Wav2Vec2’s input expectations. The model’s final hidden states were mean-pooled across time, producing a fixed-length audio feature vector for each sample.

These two feature vectors were concatenated into a single 1,280-dimensional representation, which then fed into a fully connected layer mapping to the binary classification output. To mitigate class imbalance, we computed inverse frequency weights from the training labels and applied them in the cross-entropy loss. The combined network was trained end-to-end using the Adam optimizer and a StepLR scheduler. We employed early stopping with a patience of three epochs based on validation accuracy to prevent overfitting.

Throughout training, we tracked loss curves and accuracy metrics for both training and validation splits. On convergence, the model achieved high discriminative performance, with validation accuracies consistently exceeding 90% and F1-scores above 0.88. Confusion matrices revealed that false positives (classifying real videos as fake) remained below 5%, demonstrating the model’s reliability in real-world scenarios. Visualizations of the learning curves confirmed stable training dynamics, with the audio-visual fusion model outperforming single-modality baselines by a significant margin.

Despite these successes, the audio-visual approach has inherent limitations. It relies on clear, synchronous audio; background noise, dubbing, or aggressive compression can degrade spectrogram quality and impair the Wav2Vec2 embeddings. Likewise, extremely short clips may not provide sufficient speech content for reliable audio feature extraction. Moreover, the concatenation strategy assumes equal importance of audio and visual streams, whereas more advanced fusion techniques—such as cross-modal attention—might further enhance performance. Finally, our dataset, while diverse, may not encompass every deepfake generation method, potentially limiting generalization to novel forgeries.

Nevertheless, the audio-visual module constitutes a vital pillar of our multi-modal detection pipeline. By capturing modality coherence and mapping it to a unified feature space, it complements the spatial and temporal components, together forming a robust defense against deepfake media. The final ensemble can thus leverage spatial artifacts, motion irregularities, and audio-visual misalignments in concert, yielding state-of-the-art detection accuracy across a wide range of manipulated content.
